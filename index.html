Project Summary

Students learn supersonic flow on paper but never see it.

We designed a planar Mach 2 converging-diverging nozzle, 3D-printed it in one piece from carbon-fiber PETG, and built a compressed-air test rig that measures pressure and temperature during each run. A schlieren optical system makes the shock waves in the exit jet visible, and supply pressure alone moves the jet through over-, perfectly and under-expanded flow. The rig is ready to become a hands-on Gas Dynamics lab at Tennessee Tech.

Test Rig and Instrumentation

  • Air supply: lab compressor and receiver tanks, up to 130 psig
  • Sensors: pressure transmitter and thermocouple, 3-channel LabVIEW DAQ
  • Schlieren: single 900 mm focal-length mirror, point source and knife edge

First attempt: a two-mirror Z-type system

Our first schlieren setup was a Z-type system on a 3D-printed rail, with two mirrors, a flashlight source and a phone camera. The two mirrors and the knife edge all had to line up at once, which proved too hard to keep aligned. We switched to the single-mirror layout above, which has far fewer parts to align.

Nozzle Design and Prototypes

How It Works

Squeeze the flow to Mach 1, then let it expand past the speed of sound.

Below the speed of sound, gas speeds up when the passage gets narrower. Above it, the opposite is true: supersonic gas speeds up when the passage gets wider. A converging-diverging (de Laval) nozzle uses both. The converging section accelerates the air to exactly Mach 1 at the narrowest point, the throat. The diverging section then expands it to supersonic speed. For our nozzle, an exit-to-throat area ratio of 1.73 gives Mach 2 at the exit.

The jet leaves the nozzle at its own exit pressure, which usually doesn't match the room. The mismatch forms waves that repeat down the jet as a chain of "shock diamonds":

Over-expandedpe < patm. Oblique shocks at the exit squeeze the jet inward.
Perfectly expandedpe = patm. The jet leaves cleanly with only weak waves. This is the design point, about 100 psig supply.
Under-expandedpe > patm. Expansion fans at the exit let the jet spread outward.

How Schlieren Imaging Works

Air is invisible, but a shock wave bends light. Schlieren turns that bending into brightness.

Step through the diagram with the numbered buttons. On a phone, swipe the drawing sideways to see all of it.

What the camera sees

1. A tiny light. A small, bright point of light (an LED behind a pinhole) shines at a concave mirror, the same shape as a telescope mirror.

2. The mirror focuses it. The mirror reflects the light back and focuses it to a sharp point. Because the source sits at twice the focal length, the light returns to almost the same spot. A camera placed there sees the whole mirror lit up evenly.

3. Block half the light. A razor blade, the "knife edge", slides into the focused point and blocks about half of it. The mirror dims evenly to gray. Now the system is sensitive: any ray that moves even a fraction of a millimeter at the focus changes brightness.

4. Turn on the flow. A shock compresses the air, and denser air bends light slightly, like a weak lens. Rays passing through a shock are deflected, so they land a little above or below the focus. Rays pushed into the blade get blocked and that spot turns dark. Rays pushed away from it get through and that spot turns bright. The invisible shocks show up as dark and light lines.

The deflection angles are tiny, a few thousandths of a degree, so the drawing exaggerates them. Shadowgraph, the simpler cousin, skips the knife edge. It shows only the sharpest changes, as paired light and dark lines.
